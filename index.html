01 / 10
Tabby family

Mackerel tabby

Narrow, roughly parallel stripes often run down the sides, like a little tiger in a cardigan.

Spot the details

Look for thin vertical flank stripes, facial lines, and often an M-shaped forehead marking. Stripes may break or vary.

02 / 10
Tabby family

Classic tabby

Bold, flowing swirls and broad bands make this coat look almost marbled.

Spot the details

Look for thick curved markings; the sides may show round or bullseye-like patterns. Also called blotched tabby.

03 / 10
Tabby family

Spotted tabby

Dots and ovals replace continuous stripes. Some spots form loose rows.

Spot the details

Watch for distinct spots along the flanks. Spot size and arrangement vary, and some tabbies have both broken stripes and dots.

04 / 10
Tabby family

Ticked tabby

Individual hairs show bands of color, creating a softly speckled finish.

Spot the details

The body can look almost unstriped from a distance, though facial, leg, or tail markings may still show.

05 / 10
Patchwork family

Tortoiseshell

Orange-based and dark patches blend into a unique mottled mosaic, usually with little or no white.

Spot the details

Often called a tortie. A diluted version looks blue-gray and cream. Tortie cats are usually female, though exceptions exist.

06 / 10
Patchwork family

Calico

A cheerful mix of white plus orange-based and dark patches, often in larger color blocks.

Spot the details

Also called tortoiseshell-and-white in some naming systems. The three colors can appear as black, orange and white—or diluted shades.

07 / 10
White markings

Tuxedo

Usually a dark coat with crisp white on the chest, belly, and sometimes paws or face.

Spot the details

“Tuxedo” is an informal name for one style of bicolor markings, not a distinct breed or gene of its own.

08 / 10
White markings

Bicolor

White paired with another color or pattern in patches large or small.

Spot the details

A cat can be tabby-and-white or solid-and-white. “Van” describes a mostly white look with color concentrated near the head and tail.

09 / 10
Pigment pattern

Colorpoint

A paler body with richer pigment on cooler areas: the ears, mask, paws, and tail.

Spot the details

Commonly associated with Siamese cats, but it occurs beyond that breed. Some pointed cats also show tabby stripes on the points.

10 / 10
Even color

Solid / self

One dominant color with no obvious contrasting pattern across most of the coat.

Spot the details

Black, blue-gray and cream are examples. Faint “ghost” stripes can appear, especially in young cats or certain lighting.

No coats found

Try another name, like “tabby,” “white,” or “spots.”

✳ Real coats don't always fit one box. A cat can be a spotted tabby and white, a tortie tabby (torbie), or a tabby point.

03 / Under the fur

A little coat science.

Behind every marking is a combination of pigment, patterns, and the genes influencing where color appears.

CATS
✳
ARE ART
01

Pattern isn't breed

“Tabby,” “calico,” and “tuxedo” describe coat appearance—not pedigree. Many breeds and mixed-ancestry domestic cats can share the same markings.

↗
02

Stripes begin with pigment patterns

Tabby variations involve the distribution of dark and light pigment in fur. The agouti system influences hair banding, while other genetic factors affect the visible stripe, swirl, or spot arrangement.

〰
03

Orange-and-dark patchwork is special

The orange/non-orange coat-color system is associated with the X chromosome. That's why tortoiseshell and calico coats are most often seen in females, although males with these patterns do occur.

✳
04

Cooler parts can grow darker

In colorpoint cats, temperature-sensitive pigment production creates darker color on the face, ears, paws, and tail. A kitten's coat can change as it grows.

Coat clues are only the beginning

Coat pattern ≠ cat breed.

Two cats with similar stripes may have very different backgrounds. Coat markings describe what you see, while breed identification looks more broadly at the face, ears, body proportions, fur type, and documented ancestry.

Curious about a possible breed match beyond the coat? Try the free photo-based visual identifier at WhatisMyCatBreed.com. It compares multiple visible features to suggest possible matches—not proof of breed, pedigree, or genetic ancestry.

04 / Good questions

Curiosity looks
good on you.

A few common mix-ups, cleared up.

Is tabby a cat breed?+

No. Tabby refers to a family of coat patterns, including striped, blotched, spotted and ticked forms. Cats from many breeds and mixed backgrounds may be tabbies.

What is the difference between calico and tortoiseshell?+

Both combine orange-based and dark colors. Tortoiseshell coats typically have little or no white; calico coats include significant white spotting, often making colored patches appear more separated.

Can a cat have more than one pattern?+

Yes. A tabby can also have white patches; a tortie with tabby markings is sometimes called a torbie; and a colorpoint cat can show stripes on the darker points. The finder suggests a starting label, not a complete coat classification.

Are all colorpoint cats Siamese?+

No. Colorpoint is a coat pattern seen in Siamese and other cats. Appearance alone does not establish pedigree or ancestry.

Does this tool analyze photos or identify breeds?+

No. This mini-site uses your answers to three visual questions to suggest common coat-pattern labels. It does not upload or analyze photos, infer DNA, or verify a breed.
